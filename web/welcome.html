A shared curiosity

Wanting to know the body is an old habit.

Drawn on paper, cast in wax, published as data. How anatomy is taught has changed with each era.

1543Drawn on paper

Vesalius publishes the Fabrica. Detailed woodcuts carry the structure of the body to readers across Europe.

U.S. National Library of Medicine
1700sGiven depth

Anatomical wax models flourish in Florence, teaching form and depth to students who could not dissect.

University of Florence
2007Opened as data

BodyParts3D is published in Japan: a shared, reusable model of where each structure sits and what shape it takes.

Database Center for Life Science
TodayIn your hands

Human Atlas brings those published models into the browser and shortens the distance between a learner and the knowledge.

Before you start

Questions.

How much does it cost?

Nothing. Human Atlas is open source under the MIT licence, and the anatomy data is published under Creative Commons licences. There is no account and no paid tier.

Does it work on a phone?

Yes. The controls rearrange for small screens: one finger rotates, two fingers zoom and pan, and the systems panel opens from the bottom dock. Loading the full model downloads about 33 MB of compressed geometry, so Wi‑Fi helps the first time.

Can I use it for diagnosis or treatment planning?

No. The models are reference anatomy from a single adult male and do not include every structure or variation. Use it to learn and to explain, not to make clinical decisions.
